Before I wrote software, I kept cold storage running.

That work has a quality software people talk about constantly and rarely
experience so directly: the feedback loop is brutal and immediate. A
compressor either holds temperature or it doesn't. Product either survives the
night or it spoils. There is no staging environment for a refrigeration unit at
two in the morning.

I think about that a lot now, and I have come to believe the transition was
less of a career change than it looked from the outside.

## What the work actually was

Industrial maintenance is diagnosis under pressure with incomplete
information. A unit fails. You have gauge readings, a temperature log, whatever
the operator noticed, and a system with a dozen interacting parts, any of which
could be the cause — or a symptom of the real cause somewhere else.

You learn to work backwards from the failure. You learn that the loudest symptom
is usually not the root cause. And you learn to distinguish "I fixed it" from
"it is working right now," which are not the same claim.

## Why I went back to study

The honest answer is that I kept finding the same bottleneck.

The technical problems were solvable. The problems around them — tracking which
unit was serviced when, which parts were ordered, what the recurring failures
were across a whole site — lived in notebooks and spreadsheets and people's
heads. The information existed. It just wasn't usable.

I started building small things to fix that for myself. Then I realised I
preferred building the thing that made the work legible to doing the work. So I
went to study it properly rather than continuing to improvise.

I already held a D1, so rather than starting over I am continuing to a
bachelor's in Information Systems at Universitas Subang, studying alongside work
through the Recognition of Prior Learning track. That detail matters more than
it sounds: the programme counts the years I spent diagnosing equipment as
learning, which is exactly the argument this article is making.

## What transferred

Not the domain knowledge. Nothing about refrigeration cycles helps me write
TypeScript. What transferred was the method.

**Systematic troubleshooting.** Debugging is diagnosis. Reproduce it, isolate
the variable, change one thing at a time, verify the fix rather than assuming
it. I already had that discipline; it just moved from a compressor to a stack
trace.

**System thinking.** Refrigeration taught me that components are only
meaningful in relation to each other, and that a change in one place surfaces
somewhere unexpected. That maps almost directly onto how a database schema,
an API, and a UI constrain each other.

**Respect for failure modes.** In maintenance you plan for the thing breaking,
because it will. That habit turns into validating input, handling the error
path, and not assuming the network is reliable.

**Working with non-technical people.** Operators described symptoms in their
own language, and translating that into a diagnosis was half the job. Clients
describe software the same way. "It's slow" is a symptom, not a specification.

**Documentation as maintenance.** A service log exists so the next technician —
often future you — isn't guessing. So does a README.

## What I'm learning now

- [x] PHP and MySQL — the foundation, and what shipped my first real project
- [x] Laravel — structure, migrations, and an ORM instead of hand-rolled queries
- [x] Tailwind CSS — design systems I can actually maintain
- [ ] Next.js and TypeScript — where I'm spending most of my time
- [ ] AI-assisted automation — bots and integrations that remove repetitive
      work. I'm currently building a Telegram bot with Hermes AI, deployed on
      IDCloudHost, to handle routine requests and automate repetitive tasks.
- [ ] Deployment and infrastructure beyond shared hosting

That bot is the closest thing to my old job — it's the same instinct as
building a jig so a repetitive task stops consuming attention. Automate the
predictable part, keep your attention for the part that needs judgement.

## Where this is going

I want to build systems that make messy, real-world operations legible — the
kind of work I needed and couldn't find when I was on the maintenance side.
Village information systems, records management for small offices, automation
for teams too small to have an IT department. Unglamorous software for people
who are currently doing it on paper.

The career change looks larger on a CV than it feels day to day. I traded one
set of systems for another. The diagnosis is the same.

> If you can find out why a compressor is short-cycling, you can find out why
> a request is timing out. The tools are different. The thinking is not.
